---
title: A slideshow, and another display
description: Showing a folder on a clock, in order or shuffled, and putting the picture on a television while the laptop keeps its window.
---

Going through a folder and showing it are different jobs. Going through it,
you decide when the next picture comes; showing it, the pictures come by
themselves and you watch with everyone else — often on a bigger screen than
the one you are sitting at.

## A slideshow

**`S` shows the folder one picture after another**, starting from the one on
screen. The window goes full screen and the pointer gets out of the way; move
the mouse and it comes back. `S` again, or `Esc`, stops the show and gives the
window back the way it was.

**`Shift+S` shows it in a random order.** Not a random pick at every step: the
folder is dealt like a deck, so every picture comes up once before any comes
up twice, and a new round never opens on the picture the last one ended on.
`S` and `Shift+S` switch between the two orders while the show runs.

**While it runs:**

| Key | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `Space` | pause; again to go on, with the picture on screen given its whole time again |
| `↑` `↓` | longer or shorter on each picture — 1 s up to 10 min |
| `←` `→` | back or on by hand; in a shuffled show, back through what was actually shown |
| `Esc` | stop |

The pace is a setting — **Settings → Slideshow** — and `↑` `↓` write it back,
so the next show runs at the pace this one was left at. The status line says
the show is on, at what pace, and when it is paused.

**Each picture gets its whole time.** The count starts when the picture is
fully on screen, not when it was asked for: a large file that takes a second
to decode still gets its five, and the next picture is decoded ahead so it
usually takes none. An animation plays one whole cycle before the show moves
on, up to a minute. A file that will not open is named and given its time
rather than skipped at once.

**Nothing changes under your hands.** While the box for a new name, a save or
a clean copy is open, or a crop is being framed, the show waits; it goes on
when the box is closed.

The show walks what the arrow keys walk: with `M` up, only the marked
pictures. It goes round at the end of the folder whatever the wrap setting
says — that setting is for working through a shoot, and a show for a room goes
on. A paused show costs nothing; a running one wakes the viewer once a picture
and not otherwise.

## Another display

**`D` puts the picture on another display as well** — a television, a
projector — full screen there, with nothing over it: no toolbar, no status
line, no pointer. The window at the laptop stays exactly as it was, and it
keeps the keyboard, so you go on browsing, zooming and comparing there while
the room watches. `D` again moves the picture to the next display, if there
is one, and after the last takes it away; so does `Esc`, once a show and
anything else in front have been left.

**The other display shows what you see.** The same part of the picture,
taking the same share of its screen: zoom into a face at the laptop and the
television shows that face, filling it the way it fills the laptop. Two
pictures being compared are laid out for the television's own shape, and a
blink blinks there too.

**It follows its own display.** A television in HDR shows an HDR photograph in
HDR even when the laptop's screen is standard range, and the other way round.
It draws only when what it shows changes — the toolbar coming down at the
laptop or a message fading there costs the television nothing.

**A slideshow with another display up does not take over the laptop.** The
show runs on both, and the window at the laptop stays a window: the point of
putting the picture on the other display is to keep working at this one.

If the other display goes away — unplugged, switched off — its picture is
taken away rather than moved onto the laptop over what you are doing.
Clicking the television's picture and pressing a key there does the same as
at the laptop.

Colour on both displays is converted for the profile of the primary display,
the one Windows reports for the desktop. On a television that is usually what
you want; converting for each display's own profile is planned.
